Does machine learning beat Elo in League of Legends?

I tested it on 75,830 professional games. Machine learning won, but by about 0.3%. Most of the gain came from fixing the rating system itself.

By Nahian IbnatNovember 2026Data: Oracle's Elixir, January 2020 to November 2026

Elo ratings are the default way to predict who wins a game. They are simple: every result moves the winner up and the loser down, by more when the result is a surprise. Machine learning models can use far more information, such as recent form, the strength of past opponents and experience. I wanted to know whether that extra information actually buys better predictions, and how much. (New to Elo or esports terms? The site has a short FAQ and glossary.)

To find out, I built Esports Intelligence, a research site on every professional League of Legends game recorded by Oracle's Elixir from 2020 to November 2026: 75,830 games, 1,914 teams and 10,239 players across 109 leagues.

The test

Every model was trained on games up to mid-August 2024, tuned on the following thirteen months, and then scored once on 10,087 games from 23 September 2025 to 14 November 2026 that it had never seen. Each prediction uses only games played before it.1 I score models with the Brier score, the average squared gap between the predicted probability and what happened. Lower is better, and a coin flip scores 0.25.

Significant, but small

The logistic regression has the best score, and the difference is real. Against calibrated Elo it lowers the Brier score by 0.00062, with a 95% interval of 0.00023 to 0.00104. The interval stays above zero when I resample whole series, and also when I resample whole tournaments.2

But 0.00062 is about 0.3% of the score, and both models pick the right winner about 65% of the time. Gradient boosting and random forests did no better than the logistic regression, so I left them out.

The bigger lesson was about the rating

My first Elo version put every team on one scale. It ranked teams from small regional leagues above the best teams in Korea and China, because those teams beat their own neighbours and rarely played anyone else. Elo points only move between leagues when their teams meet, so a single scale was not telling the truth.

The fix was to give each league its own level, learned from the games between leagues, with each team rated relative to its league. That one change lowered the Brier score by 0.0054, much more than anything the machine learning added afterwards.

Statistically significant and worth it are different questions. A model can be clearly better and still not matter much in practice.

What else is on the site

  • Prediction lab: calibration, uncertainty intervals, and a prediction for any two teams with the reasons behind it.
  • Match centre: every series and game since 2020.
  • Statistics: competitive balance by league and season (Gini, HHI, Lorenz curves). The best regular-season team went on to win the title in 52% of 408 splits.
  • Data quality report: what was excluded and why.
  • FAQ and glossary: plain-language definitions of Elo, the Brier score, series and other terms, with a small Elo calculator to try.

How this fits with earlier work

I am not the first to ask this question. Riot Games and AWS launched the LoL Esports Global Power Rankings at Worlds 2024. Their rating mixes a team Elo with a league Elo, weighted 80/20, and results at MSI and Worlds move the league Elo. That is close to the league-level Elo described above. Riot reports about 65% accuracy, and my Elo reaches 64.7% on my test games. The two numbers come from different games, so they are not a direct comparison, but they point the same way.

Machine learning on professional League games has been tried too. Jadowski and Cunningham (2022) correctly predicted 67% of 306 international games from 2020. A 2021 SBGames paper trained several classifiers on the same Oracle's Elixir data I use. PandaSkill (De Bois et al., 2025) rates players from their in-game statistics, adds a rating for each region, and reports better predictions than Elo and TrueSkill over five years of pro matches. Accuracy figures across these studies come from different games and periods, so they cannot be ranked against each other.

What this project adds is mostly in how the models are checked, not in the models themselves: a split by date with a test period never used for tuning, constant baselines, uncertainty intervals that resample whole series, and a clear line between a gain that is statistically real and one that matters. Under those checks, the extra gain from machine learning on pre-game features is small, and league structure does most of the work. PandaSkill's gains come from player-level game data, which my ratings do not use, so the two results do not conflict. The data, code and data quality report are all public, so anyone can rerun the test.

Limits

The source has no series identifier, so series are rebuilt from game times. Ratings are only comparable between leagues that play each other often. The models are fixed at the start of the test period, and there is only one test period, so the result is one look at the future, not a guarantee.

The full report, with every number and the code behind it, is on the site and on GitHub.

Notes
  1. The data is split by date, never at random: training until 11 August 2024, validation until 22 September 2025, test after that. Every feature (Elo gap, recent form, overall win rate, strength of past opponents, experience) uses only games played earlier.
  2. Paired bootstrap with 2,000 resamples. Games from the same series are resampled together, because they are not independent.
  3. Built with Python (pandas, scikit-learn), FastAPI, PostgreSQL and Next.js. Data from Oracle's Elixir by Tim Sevenhuysen. Game statistics are the property of Riot Games; this project is not affiliated with Riot Games.
